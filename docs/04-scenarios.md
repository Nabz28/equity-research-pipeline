# Scenarios, weighted value, and "is it priced in"

## Three scenarios, with probabilities that sum to one

Bull, base, bear. Each one needs four things: a probability, a price, the percentage
change from today, and the arithmetic. The arithmetic is usually the same shape:

```
full year profit  x  the multiple the market pays  /  shares outstanding  =  price
```

Write that line on the slide. A target with no arithmetic behind it is a wish, and a
reader who can see the arithmetic can disagree with one input instead of the whole page.

Probabilities have to be uneven. 33/33/33 means you have not thought about it. If the bear
case needs a commodity to fall 25% after it already rose 25%, that is not a 33% event.

Then state the weighted value and compare it to the current price. If the weighted value
is 10% above the price and the stop is 6% below it, that is the trade. If it is 2% above,
say so and recommend nothing.

## Is it priced in

This is the section readers actually pay for, and the one most notes skip. Three tests.

**Test 1. Compare the stock to its driver.** If the story is a commodity, plot the stock's
move against the commodity's move over the same window. A planter up 31% while its own
output price is up 2% has already been paid for the story. That gap is the answer.

**Test 2. Compare the multiple to the peer that earns more.** Cheap on book value means
nothing on its own. A company at 0.65 times book earning 8% on equity is not cheap next to
a peer at 0.69 times book earning 15%. Return on equity is what you are buying.

**Test 3. Find the number nobody has modelled.** This is where the edge is. Look for a mix
shift, a contract roll, a tariff change or a capacity addition that is visible in the
filings and absent from every published note. One real example: a utility selling the same
kilowatt hour to an industrial customer for 64% more than to the state offtaker, with the
state volume falling every year. Shifting it adds about 15% to EBITDA with no capex, and
no note covered it.

If all three tests say priced in, the honest output is "do not buy this here" plus a level
at which you would. That is a useful note. A forced buy call is not.

## Takeover and tender situations

For a control deal, the mechanics decide the direction before any valuation does. Compute
the mandatory tender floor from the rule that applies, and work out whether the buyer
actually crosses into control. Then ask who benefits from a higher price and who benefits
from a lower one. See [07-idx-playbook.md](07-idx-playbook.md) for the Indonesian version,
where the floor is set by a 90 day window that locks on announcement.

The general principle travels: once the window locks, pushing the price down afterwards
does not lower the tender price for that announcement. So a "they will crush it to buy
cheap" thesis only works if a fresh announcement is still to come.
